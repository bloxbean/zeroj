Proof of solvency with hidden balances

View Markdown ↗

The proof of reserves page hides every customer balance but publishes the total liabilities. Sometimes the total is sensitive too: it tells competitors how big the exchange is and how fast it’s growing. And a Merkle inclusion path shows a customer hashes of their neighbours’ leaves.

This variant hides the total as well. Each customer balance becomes a Pedersen commitment, a sealed number that can still be added up. The exchange proves the hidden balances fit under a reserve it locks on-chain. Each customer opens their own entry from the chain, and an auditor opens only the total. It follows the Provisions approach (Dagher et al., CCS 2015).

The zero-knowledge idea

Section titled “The zero-knowledge idea”

The exchange proves “these committed balances are each between 0 and 2⁶⁴, and together they add up to at most the reserve locked in this vault”, without revealing any balance or the total.

Two properties of Pedersen commitments make this work. (See Commitments & encryption for an interactive introduction.)

  • Hiding and binding. A commitment C = v·G + r·H with a random 252-bit blinding r reveals nothing about the balance v, and the exchange can’t later claim it holds a different number. The proof opens every commitment inside the circuit, so it sums exactly the published balances.
  • Adding up. Commitments add: C₁ + C₂ + C₃ + C₄ is the commitment to the total, with the sum of the blindings. Anyone can compute that point from the chain. Only someone given its opening can read the total, and the exchange gives that opening to one auditor.

Each customer gets their own opening the same way, as a small encrypted delivery next to their entry in the datum. They read it with their viewing key, so what they check comes from the chain, not from the exchange’s say-so. See Share a value with chosen readers.

How it works

Section titled “How it works”
  1. The book. At the start of a period the exchange has its customer balances. In the demo, Alice 500, Bob 1,200, Carol 300 and Dave 0 ADA. Each customer receives a fresh 32-byte salt for the period with their account statement.
  2. Commit and deliver. For each customer the exchange builds an entry: a salted id hash blake2b_256(I2OSP2(len(id)) ‖ id ‖ salt) (the length as two big-endian bytes), the commitment C_i to the balance, and the opening (v_i, r_i) encrypted to the customer’s viewing key (89 bytes). It also encrypts the opening of the sum, (L, Σr_i mod l), to the auditor’s registered viewing key. Every attestation draws a new salt and a new blinding for every customer and shuffles the entries into a fresh random order, which the proof and the deliveries follow too.
  3. Prove and lock. The exchange proves Σ v_i ≤ R with Groth16. It then locks R lovelace in the period’s vault with the entries as the inline datum, before the period starts. The vault verifies the proof against the datum’s commitments and the locked amount.
  4. Customers check. During the period, each customer derives the period’s vault script themselves, reads every live attestation, and looks for their idHash. Then they open their delivery with their own key and compare the result with their statement.
  5. The auditor opens the total. The auditor adds the on-chain commitments and opens the sum with its own delivery. It learns L and checks it against R, without learning any single balance.
  6. Release. Once the period is over, the exchange spends the vault and burns the attestation token. Nothing can be added or released while the period runs, so every attestation in the period is locked at the same time and the same reserve can’t back two of them in turn.

The circuit

Section titled “The circuit”

This is the demo’s HiddenLiabilitySolvencyProof, with the constructor check trimmed. It opens each commitment in the circuit and sums the hidden balances. The public inputs are [R, u₁…u_N, v₁…v_N]: the reserve and the coordinates of each commitment, read from the datum.

  • @UInt(bits = 64) on the balances rules out a negative “balance” that would quietly shrink the total. With at most 16 balances below 2⁶⁴, the sum can’t wrap around the field.
  • The blinding is a 252-bit secret, as ZeroJ requires for a hiding commitment. See the Pedersen guide.
  • With four customers the circuit has 10,042 constraints.

Customers and the auditor check

Section titled “Customers and the auditor check”

Both checks use only chain data and the reader’s own viewing key. They run with ZeroJ’s confidential-note API, NoteScanner. The result of a customer’s check is one of five outcomes:

ResultMeaning
LISTED_ONCE_CORRECTTheir idHash appears exactly once, and the delivery opens to their balance
MISSINGNo entry with their idHash: they were left out
LISTED_TWICETheir idHash appears more than once in the period
UNOPENABLEThe entry is there, but its delivery doesn’t open with their key
WRONG_BALANCEThe delivery opens, but to a different balance

Anything other than LISTED_ONCE_CORRECT is evidence against the exchange. In particular the vault can only check that a delivery is 89 bytes long, not that it decrypts. An exchange can attest with a garbage delivery, but the customer it targets finds out.

These snippets are simplified from the demo’s SolvencyAttestation. The commitments and deliveries come from the attestation datum.

open returns empty when the delivery doesn’t open, or when the decrypted opening doesn’t recompute the commitment. The exchange side builds each delivery with ConfidentialNotes.seal. See the confidential notes guide.

On Cardano

Section titled “On Cardano”

The demo’s SolvencyVault is a JuLC multi-validator. One script is both the attestation minting policy and the vault address. Its parameters fix the exchange’s key hash, the number of entries, the period [periodStart, periodEnd] and the verification key, so each period has its own vault script.

  • Attest (mint). The validity range must end at or before periodStart, and the exchange must sign. Exactly one attestation token goes to one output at the vault’s exact address, holding only lovelace plus the token. The vault checks the datum Attestation([Entry(idHash, u, v, delivery)] × N, auditorDelivery): 32-byte id hashes, canonical coordinates and 89-byte deliveries. It then builds the public inputs from the locked lovelace and the datum’s coordinates and runs Groth16BLS12381Lib.verify.
  • Release (spend and burn). The validity range must start at or after periodEnd, the exchange signs, and the attestation token is burned.

The demo measured the attest transaction with four customers at about 4.41 billion CPU steps and 1.25 million memory units in the JuLC VM. That was before two later cost reductions: the demo’s datum-access optimization (zeroj-usecases commit 814b2f0) and ZeroJ’s cheaper on-chain Groth16 verifier (ZeroJ PRs #85 and #86). Each extra customer adds two public inputs, and the demo’s own estimate puts the per-transaction limit at about 19 customers per attestation. A larger book needs batching.

Like every validator, this one checks a proof against the data it can see. It doesn’t know where the reserves came from or that every customer was included. Those gaps are listed below. See Application security.

Security considerations

Section titled “Security considerations”
  • Borrowed reserves. The vault proves the funds are locked for the period, not that the exchange owns them. It could borrow them for exactly that window.
  • Who ran the setup matters. If the exchange ran the Groth16 setup itself, it could forge a proof. The verification key must come from a ceremony the exchange can’t subvert. The demo uses a single-party development setup (Trusted setup, explained).
  • Omissions are caught only by customers who check. Leaving someone out, or understating them, is detected only if that customer runs their check. Make the check easy and automatic in the wallet.
  • The customer count is public. Pad with commitments to 0, which look like any other commitment, to hide the real N.
  • The auditor learns one subtotal per attestation. If a period were split into several attestations, the auditor would learn each one’s total, and an attestation holding one real customer plus zero-padding would reveal that customer’s balance. The demo makes exactly one attestation per period.
  • Fresh per attestation. Reusing a salt, a blinding or an entry’s position would link a customer’s entries across periods. An earlier version of the demo reused every salt and blinding when it re-attested, and kept entries in book order. Now each attestation draws fresh salts and blindings and shuffles the entries with SecureRandom, so two attestations of the same book share no id hash, no commitment and no position. A customer finds their entry by this period’s salted id hash, never by position.
  • Outside the period the attestation proves nothing. It covers [periodStart, periodEnd] only.
  • The exchange’s key. The exchange’s signature makes an attestation the exchange’s, so the demo generates its wallet key randomly and keeps it, owner-only, in the data directory next to its deployment state. If that state exists but the key is missing or doesn’t match, the demo stops instead of creating a new key.
  • Demo custody and operator view. The demo server holds every wallet’s keys and scans the chain on everyone’s behalf, and its API is unauthenticated. GET /api/solvency is the public view (the period, the vault and its on-chain entries, with no customer ids or balances). GET /api/solvency/operator is the exchange’s private book, shown in the UI’s “Private” panel. That is the demo operator’s view: privacy here holds against chain and mempool observers, not against the demo server. Real customers scan in their own wallet. The off-circuit commitment and sealing code is for offline, isolated use; see the guides for the exact rules.
  • Maturity. ZeroJ’s Pedersen and confidential-note APIs are experimental and haven’t been externally audited. Treat this as a blueprint, not a production system.

Try it

Section titled “Try it”

The demo is tab “C. Hidden-liability solvency” in the pedersen-commitments app in zeroj-usecases. With Yaci DevKit running (see Run the demos):

To run it without Docker, use Java 25 and ./gradlew -p pedersen-commitments bootRun -PwithFrontend from the repository root.

The first start compiles the circuits and runs development setups, which takes a few minutes. In the tab you can edit the book, attest with locked reserves for a period that starts about 45 seconds later and lasts 180 seconds, run each customer’s check, and let the auditor open the total. Try to cheat: lock less than the book (no proof exists), give one customer a garbage delivery (their check reports UNOPENABLE), or attest again or release inside the period (the vault rejects it).

Design notes: ADR-0006 (demo C) and ADR-0007 (on-chain deliveries) in the zeroj-usecases docs/adr directory.
