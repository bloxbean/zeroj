Commitments & encryption, explained

View Markdown ↗

A zero-knowledge proof answers a question about secret data. Often that’s all you need: “is this person over 18?” But many applications also have to keep a secret value on a public ledger: a balance, a salary, a bid, a vote. Somebody needs to check it now, somebody else may need to read it later, and everyone else should see nothing.

Four tools cover almost every case. This page explains each one with an everyday picture, and Choose how to disclose helps you pick between them.

ToolEveryday pictureWhat it gives you
CommitmentA sealed envelopePublish a value without revealing it; prove facts about it; open it later
Encryption to a keyA lockbox with a slotAnyone can put a value in; only the key holder can read it
Encrypted deliveryA letter to named readersHand the opening of a commitment to the owner and an auditor, on-chain
Shared key (threshold)A safe that needs two of three keysNobody alone can decrypt; a quorum can

Commitments: a sealed envelope

Section titled “Commitments: a sealed envelope”

A commitment lets you fix a value now and reveal it later, or never. ZeroJ uses Pedersen commitments. To commit to a value v, you pick a large random number r, the blinding, and publish one point on an elliptic curve:

G and H are two fixed public points. You don’t need to follow the math to use it; three properties matter:

  • Hiding. Because r is random, C reveals nothing about v. Two commitments to the same amount look unrelated.
  • Binding. Once you publish C, you can’t later claim it held a different value. Finding a second opening is as hard as breaking the curve.
  • Adding. Commitments add up: C(a, r) + C(b, s) = C(a + b, r + s). A ledger can total balances, or check that inputs equal outputs, without seeing any single amount.

Step through it. Drag the amount, roll a new blinding, and try opening the envelope to a different number.

A commitment on its own only lets you open or not open. Its real power comes with a zero-knowledge proof over the hidden value: “the amount in C is at least 1,000”, “these three commitments add up to that one”, “this commitment holds a 0 or a 1”. The proof reveals nothing else, and C stays sealed.

Sums wrap around

Commitment arithmetic wraps around at a huge number, about 2²⁵². A cheat could hide a “negative” amount that looks like an enormous positive one, so a sum of commitments proves nothing about real totals until every value is proven to be in range. ZeroJ’s circuit gadgets check ranges and refuse layouts that could wrap. See Pedersen commitments.

Encryption to a key: a lockbox with a slot

Section titled “Encryption to a key: a lockbox with a slot”

A commitment can only be opened by someone who knows the opening. Sometimes a third party must be able to read the value without the owner’s cooperation: a tax authority reading salaries, an auctioneer reading bids, election trustees counting votes. For that you encrypt the value to their public key.

ZeroJ uses exponential ElGamal on the same curve. A ciphertext is two points, and it has two useful properties:

  • Fresh randomness every time, so the same value encrypted twice gives unrelated ciphertexts.
  • Adding. Ciphertexts add up like commitments, so a ledger can keep an encrypted running total.

The catch: decrypting ends with a lookup, so it only works for small numbers, such as votes or amounts split into 32-bit pieces (called limbs). Decrypting a 64-bit amount directly would take too long.

Encryption and commitments work well together. A proof can show “this ciphertext encrypts the same amount as this commitment”, so the auditor’s copy can’t be faked. That’s how the sealed-bid auction forces every bid to be readable by the auctioneer, and how confidential payroll stops an employer from under-reporting a salary.

Encrypted delivery: a letter to named readers

Section titled “Encrypted delivery: a letter to named readers”

Suppose a payment is a commitment on-chain. Its owner needs the opening, (v, r), to spend it later, and an auditor may need it too. You could send it privately, but then a lost message means lost money. A confidential note puts the opening on-chain, encrypted separately to each reader.

  • Each reader has a viewing key. The sender seals the opening for each reader with a fresh one-time key, the way Zcash notes are encrypted.
  • A reader scans the chain, opens the deliveries addressed to them, and accepts a note only if the opening recomputes the commitment. A garbage delivery can’t trick them; it shows up as “unopenable”.
  • Everyone else sees the commitment and a few bytes of ciphertext per reader (89 bytes each).

Delivery is about convenience and recovery: a lost wallet can rebuild its balance from the chain. On its own it doesn’t force the sender to tell the auditor the truth; pair it with an encrypted amount and a proof when that matters. See Confidential notes.

Shared keys: no single reader

Section titled “Shared keys: no single reader”

A single auditor or trustee is a single point of failure: they could read everything, or lose the key. Threshold decryption splits the secret key among n trustees so that any t + 1 of them can decrypt together, and t or fewer learn nothing.

The trustees create the key together with a distributed key generation (DKG): each one deals shares to the others, and the full secret key never exists anywhere. ZeroJ implements a well-studied DKG (Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk and Rabin) and delivers the private shares over a public board with HPKE (RFC 9180) encryption. A typical policy is: decrypt only the final total, never a single ballot. See Threshold decryption.

What these tools don’t do

Section titled “What these tools don’t do”
  • They don’t authorize anything. A valid opening or proof says nothing about who may spend a UTxO. Validators still check signatures, replay and the transaction itself. See Application security.
  • They don’t hide the transaction graph. Who pays whom, and when, is still visible on Cardano unless the application hides it separately.
  • Key holders are trusted with what they can read. An auditor who can decrypt can also leak. Threshold keys reduce that risk; they don’t remove it.
